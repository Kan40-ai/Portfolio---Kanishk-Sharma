About

The bridge, not the pivot.

Most people building AI products right now come from one of two directions: engineers who learned to ship, or product people who learned to prompt. I spent eight years doing the harder, slower version of the second thing first — as a business analyst and Scaled Agile product owner inside organizations where a bad requirement doesn't just mean a bad sprint, it means a broken payment rail or a stalled stock-exchange migration.

I ran discovery and delivery for a card-issuing platform used across the Nordic region, a data migration for one of the largest U.S. brokerages, and a systems integration for an Australian government department — learning, project after project, how to turn a stakeholder's vague, half-formed ask into something a team could actually build and measure.

Now I'm doing the same work one layer down the stack. As a Data Science graduate student at the University of North Texas, I design and build the AI systems myself — the routing logic, the evaluation harness, the failure-mode analysis — rather than writing the requirements and handing them to someone else. The scoping instinct is the same skill. I've just moved closer to the code.

I don't see "AI Product Manager" and "AI Engineer" as two different career tracks. I see them as two ends of the same sentence: someone has to know what to build, and someone has to know it actually works.

The Ask Nobody Could Quite Articulate

Credit unions that want to buy or sell a loan participation still do it largely by spreadsheet and phone call: finding a counterparty, sizing the fit, and benchmarking against NCUA call-report data are three separate manual efforts with no systematic way to score a match. The client — an ALM advisory firm serving credit unions across Texas — had a rough sense that "an AI assistant" could help, but no scoped brief. There was no requirements document to inherit. That had to come first.

The live dashboard — persona-aware routing (top right), six active tool workflows, and the co-pilot chat panel running a real counterparty-matching conversation.
Turning "Build Us an AI Assistant" Into a Spec PM SKILL

I ran the engagement as a weekly agile loop with the client, translating each round of feedback into a progressively more formal specification. The output wasn't a slide deck — it was a working query taxonomy: 18 distinct query types across two core workflows (10 for counterparty matching, 8 for loan-tape boxing), each with its own trigger phrases, whether it re-runs the underlying pipeline or reuses session state, and its exact output format. That taxonomy became the actual engineering spec.

For example, distinguishing a fresh "find me buyers" request (full pipeline run, requires a staged 5300 filing) from a "show me only Texas sellers" request (re-rank existing results, no re-run) from a "what if I tighten FICO to 720+" request (the LLM reasons over existing summary stats, touching nothing) is what let the same conversational surface support one-shot analysis and open-ended exploration without reprocessing data on every turn.

Four Layers, and the LLM Is the Last Resort ENGINEERING SKILL

Rather than routing every message through an LLM, I built a four-layer pipeline where the model only speaks when nothing upstream already knows the answer:

  • Greeting intercept — hardcoded pattern match, zero LLM cost for the highest-frequency message type.
  • Seven pre-router rules — priority-ordered, persona-aware short-circuits for unambiguous patterns (report follow-ups, active multi-phase sessions, CU-ID-plus-verb routing) that would otherwise risk LLM misclassification.
  • LLM classifier — a persona-conditioned Groq call, reached only when no rule matches, returning a strict JSON intent with a hard fallback so a malformed response or API timeout never crashes the session.
  • Six dispatched routes — Matching Engine, Loan Boxing, Knowledge Base, Document QA, Market Data, and General Assistance, each with its own sub-paths and explicit failure handling.

On the data side, loan tapes rarely arrive with standardized column headers. I built a two-stage mapper: exact-match against a field catalog first, then a semantic fallback using sentence-transformer embeddings (all-mpnet-base-v2) and cosine similarity for anything unmapped — so the pipeline degrades gracefully instead of failing on a renamed column.

The Gap Nobody Was Measuring

Deployed AI tutors typically assess trustworthiness after generation, if at all — LLMs and RAG improve fluency and factual grounding, but neither guarantees pedagogical alignment, fairness, safety, or age-appropriateness on their own. Our research frames this as the Trustworthiness Gap in educational AI. The original brief was a few sentences from a faculty advisor: build something that tutors responsibly. There was no architecture, no agent count, no evaluation plan — just a direction.

Figure 1 from the paper — the full four-layer pipeline, six trust agents, and the severity-weighted aggregation cascade that decides BLOCK / WARN / LOG / PASS.
Refusing to Ship "Trustworthy" as One Bolt-On Filter PM SKILL

The first real product decision was refusing to treat "trustworthy" as a single safety filter bolted onto the end. I scoped it into six distinct, independently measurable dimensions — Factual Accuracy, Transparency, Pedagogical Alignment, Bias & Fairness, Safety & Ethics, Age-Appropriateness — and assigned each to its own dedicated verification agent, checked at different points in the pipeline (before generation, during, and after) rather than one model call judging everything at once.

That scoping decision is what made the system testable: each agent has its own pass/fail criteria and its own named metrics, instead of one opaque "is this response okay?" judgment nobody could debug.

Six Judges Who Never Talk to Each Other ENGINEERING SKILL

A learner query passes through four layers — Governance (persona and policy detection), Capability (intent routing via COURSE_NLP / YOUTUBE_SEARCH / GENERAL_ASSISTANCE), Trust Enforcement (six independent LLM-as-Judge agents), and Accountability (verified delivery plus a full audit log).

Each trust agent runs in isolation — no agent sees another's verdict — and the final decision is a strict severity cascade, not a vote:

  • BLOCK if the Safety Gatekeeper (Agent 5) or Developmental Guard (Agent 6) fails — a pre-generation regex firewall plus an LLM safety judge, so unsafe content never even reaches generation.
  • WARN if Factual Accuracy, Transparency, or Bias & Fairness fails.
  • LOG if Pedagogical Alignment fails — routed to an educator review queue rather than blocking delivery.
  • PASS otherwise.

That was a deliberate trade-off: a system that lets four good scores outvote one safety failure isn't one I'd call trustworthy, even if averaging would look "fairer" on paper. Retrieval itself is hybrid dense + sparse — all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings fused with BM25 via reciprocal rank fusion against a Pinecone index, reranked with a BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3 cross-encoder before the grounded response is generated.

Proving the Trust Layer Actually Does Something PM SKILL

We built EduTrust-Eval, extending RAGAS/TruLens-style factual-grounding metrics with four education-specific trust dimensions no prior framework covers. Twenty-five structured prompts compared three conditions — an ungrounded LLM baseline, a RAG-only baseline, and the full EthosAI pipeline:

Factual Accuracy0.0000.8001.000

EthosAI scored highest on every dimension — a 120% improvement over the ungrounded LLM baseline and 18.5% over RAG-only, both statistically significant (p=0.0008 and p=0.0117, large effect sizes d=1.34 and d=1.70). The sharper finding: RAG-only was not significantly better than the plain LLM baseline at all (p=0.2344) — retrieval alone barely moved the needle on trust; the enforcement layer is what actually mattered. On the two Hard Block dimensions, both baselines failed every adversarial prompt thrown at them, while EthosAI's pre-generation safety firewall correctly blocked all of them.
